Camping in Hawaii: Volcanoes, Rainforest Valleys and Island Beach Parks

Camping in Hawaii is unlike camping anywhere else in the United States. There is almost no free dispersed camping, and nearly every site needs a permit booked in advance — but the reward is camping on the rim of a volcano, in a rainforest valley, or on a black-sand beach under the trade winds. The islands are warm all year, so the seasons matter less than the rain: the windward sides are wetter, the leeward sides drier, and a rain jacket belongs in your pack on every island.

Volcanoes National Park and Haleakala

Hawaii's two national parks both offer camping, and both need planning. Hawaii Volcanoes National Park on the Big Island has the Namakanipaio campground, a grassy, eucalyptus-shaded site near the summit, plus backcountry camping for hikers who want to cross the lava fields — check current volcanic conditions before you go, because closures change with the activity. On Maui, Haleakala National Park offers the Hosmer Grove campground near the summit and the remote Kipahulu campground on the wet, green coast, reached by the Hana Highway. Both parks are high altitude at the summit, so nights are cold — bring a warm sleeping bag even when the beach is 80 degrees.

State parks, beach parks and permits

Beyond the national parks, Hawaii's state parks hold some of the best camping in the islands. Wai'anapanapa State Park on Maui pairs a black-sand beach with a small campground and a coastal trail past sea arches and blowholes. On Kauai, Polihale State Park offers beach camping at the end of a long dirt road, and Koke'e State Park in the mountains above it has cabins and campgrounds in the rainforest. Many county beach parks also allow camping with a permit. Permits are the key to all of it: book through the state parks system as early as the window allows, because popular sites sell out months ahead, and never camp without one — fines are real, and many beach parks close at night.

Planning your Hawaii trip

Hawaii camping takes more preparation than a mainland trip, so start early: permits first, then flights and a rental car, then gear. The CampAndTrailGuide gear guide covers what to pack when your trip spans a volcano summit and a beach in the same day, and the trail news digest keeps you up to date on closures, weather, and seasonal conditions. Start your planning at the CampAndTrailGuide homepage, respect cultural sites and kapu areas, leave no trace on the fragile island landscapes, and check ocean conditions before any swim — Hawaii rewards campers who plan carefully and treat the islands with respect.

Hawaii is a state of permits and payoffs: a little paperwork buys you a volcano rim, a black-sand beach, and a rainforest valley. Plan early, pack for two climates, and you will find camping like nowhere else in the country.
